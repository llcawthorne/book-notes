# No Excuses: Existentialism and the Meaning of Life

Nietzsche is ultimately a virtue ethicist in the classical Greek tradition.
The master morality holds in high regard the excellences that promote
flourishing. The slave morality is one of resentment posed as a stance
against what the masters hold dear. Christianity took a stance against
wealth because the masters held it in high regard. My own take, but for all
the talk of the Ubermensch creating his own morality, it is likely he would
act in his own self-interest, and due to non-zero-sumness would find
cooperation and "being good" worthwhile; he just wouldn't value reactionary
morals promoted by the slave. For all his talk of immorality, it's clear
from his life that Nietzsche was a good person and good friend, and he
believed philosophers should embody their philosophy. Indeed, an Ubermensch
loves himself and others greatly and lives life unselfishly with a kind and
generous mentality, forgetting himself. Indeed, the state in which we hurt
others is seldom as agreeable as that in which we benefit others.
Nietzschean morality is no harsh LaVeyan construction.

The lecture title "No Excuses" comes from Sartre's concept of Absolute
Freedom. Even if chained in a dungeon, you are absolutely free. Clearly you
aren't free to leave. But you are free to form your own opinion of the
facts, no matter what it may be. You might consider your punishment well
earned and focus on penance. Or you might consider it unjust and rage
against your oppressors. On the other hand, you could consider it the
perfect opportunity to be the model prisoner and act as such. No matter
what, you choose your interpretation and reaction. Even emotions are a
choice in what is consciously indulged and resisted, but on this he may be
wrong. We don't choose war, but we get the war we deserve by acting as good
or bad people within our predetermined background events. Your feelings
about events magically transform the world; the sour grapes really are
sour. Dennett would approve of his stance that consciousness is freedom.

Another important concept of Sartre's is "Bad Faith." It's a bit subtle,
but basically you have the facticity of yourself, just the facts, history,
and the transcendent self, what you aspire to and your personal choice. If
your transcendent self fails to accept the facts, you are acting in "Bad
Faith." For a mundane example, I could aspire to be a champion basketball
player, but the facts say I'm 5'11" and almost 50 years old. If I aspired to
be that champion, it would be in bad faith. For a more serious example, if
your actions are immoral but you aspire to be and think of yourself as a
good person, it is in bad faith. It is also in bad faith to define yourself
as what other people think or even in rebellion to what other people think.
