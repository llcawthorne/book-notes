# Existentialism and the Authentic Life

- Despite his pain from migraines, seizures, and lack of fame during his
  lifetime (many of his books sold less than 1000 copies despite being
  celebrated posthumously), Nietzsche embraced the idea of the eternal
  recurrence and Amor fati.
- Nietzsche was loath to point out examples of the Ubermensch, since one
  shouldn't base their life on that of another but create their own unique
  existence. However the term is considered applicable to Julius Caesar,
  Alexander the Great, Homer, and Goethe, so it applies to both military and
  poetic genius. The Ubermensch stretches to overcome themselves and become a
  better person, like the tightrope walker actively seeking to not fall into the
  abyss. Nietzsche thought directing the Will to rise to challenges an integral
  part of becoming, and a friend or lover that tries to remove hindrances does a
  disservice.
- Nietzsche found great value in friendship. Good friends inspire each other
  towards the ideal and have the courage to challenge each other if one is going
  astray.
- Heidegger and Ortega were both phenomenologists that liked to make up words.
  Heidegger is notoriously difficult but his ideas influenced later
  existentialists. The idea of phenomenology is that philosophers study
  phenomena, and Heidegger in particular asserted there is no study of
  phenomena without studying it in relation to the self. Both philosophers
  believed we are thrown into the world against our will, and Ortega insisted
  this is why we philosophize, to learn about the realm we were thrust into.
  Both stressed the idea of authenticity and being true to self, whatever that
  means for the individual. Ortega maintained each of us is responsible for his
  own survival and giving his life shape, but those characteristics are
  revealed in how we play the game of life, interacting with other people and
  objects.

- Albert Camus defined the Absurd as the fundamental conflict between humanity's
  innate desire for inherent meaning, purpose, and clarity, and the cold,
  silent, and indifferent universe that refuses to provide it. It is not the
  world itself that is absurd, but the inescapable clash between our minds and
  reality.
- In his 1942 essay The Myth of Sisyphus, Camus uses Greek mythology to
  illustrate this concept:
  - The Absurd Condition: Sisyphus is condemned by the gods to endlessly roll a
    massive boulder up a mountain, only to watch it roll back down to the bottom
    for eternity. Camus uses this as the ultimate metaphor for the human
    condition: waking up, going to work, eating, sleeping, and repeating the
    cycle, a life largely composed of repetitive, often futile tasks in pursuit
    of no lasting objective.
  - The Moment of Consciousness: The absurdity truly takes hold on Sisyphus's
    walk back down the mountain to retrieve the rock. In this moment, he is
    fully lucid and conscious of his fate. He recognizes the utter pointlessness
    of his task, yet he chooses to keep walking down and pushing the rock
    anyway.
  - The Absurd Revolt: Camus argues that instead of succumbing to despair or
    seeking false hope (like religion or suicide), one should live in a state of
    open-eyed revolt. By fiercely embracing his meaningless, relentless task
    with full awareness, Sisyphus strips the gods of their power to punish him.
  - The Conclusion: Camus famously concludes: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."
    Joy is found not in achieving a final, transcendent goal, but in the fierce,
    passionate struggle of living itself.
- (Back to the lectures.) While Myth of Sisyphus's focus is on solitary
  rebellion, The Plague focuses on shared rebellion in the direction of
  solidarity and participation, especially in the literal reading of the plague
  being a disease. The Plague can be read on several levels and Camus believes
  it to be his most anti-Christian work. Camus thought hoping for something
  better in the afterlife didn't justify giving up on making this life less
  hellish. Camus also disagreed with the Christian view that people are to blame
  for phenomena like plagues. The plague is also a meditation on fascism and a
  chronicle of the resistance. One problem with looking at the plague as a
  metaphor for fascism is the fact that fascism has its roots in humans. Unlike
  the plague, which is a natural force where our struggles are in vain and our
  powerlessness absurd, fascism was caused by people and was defeated by people.
- Like Hegel, Sartre believed we only become a self in relation to others.
  Sartre is known for the gloomy conclusion "Hell is other people." But he
  believed loneliness was a worse fate than being with others.
- You are more than your vocation, and it is "bad faith" to focus entirely on
  your role and forget it's your free choice every morning to assume the role
  and there are other dimensions to your existence. "I am..." "A Senior Software
  Engineer", "a father", "a good man." No matter how many of these attributes
  you list, you are missing something essential to what it means to be me. Even
  the strictest profession leaves room for self-expression. Instead of a good
  waiter, you can choose to be good and playful. We should also respect
  others' choice of self-expression and not treat them as an object. Sartre
  believed we chose what we become but at the same time are bound to make a
  choice in line with the facts of, or historicity of, our situation. You
  become what you are in the context of what others have made of you. Sartre
  thought it "bad faith" to do nothing to change your situation but also "bad
  faith" to ignore the limits around you. You have to be aware of the price you
  pay for change. There is definite tension between freedom and the facts of our
  lives.
- One of Beauvoir's most important philosophical messages is that freedom
  without responsibility is meaningless. Being unattached to commitments stifles
  action, and we are as responsible for doing nothing as doing something. Doing
  nothing means we are nothing. Only by committing ourselves and engaging in the
  world with vitality and love can we create an authentic and meaningful life.
