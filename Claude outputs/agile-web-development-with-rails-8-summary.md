# Coming to Rails 8 from JEE and JSF

I just finished *Agile Web Development with Rails 8*, worked through the whole Depot/Storefront tutorial (auth, i18n, Action Mailer and Action Mailbox, hundreds of passing tests), and read it the way I read most things outside my primary focus right now: as evening light reading alongside a much more deliberate push through Haskell. Going in, I already knew the reputation. Rails is the "Cadillac" of web frameworks, all the bells and whistles included. Coming at it from a mostly JEE/JSF background (with a little Angular mixed in) at work, I wanted to see if that reputation held up in 2026. It did.

## Batteries included, actually included

The thing that struck me early was how much Rails just hands you. A rich text editor is built in. I had to bolt one onto my own JEE app. Email isn't just "send an SMTP message and hope," it's a whole system: Action Mailer for outgoing mail with a proper test-mode delivery backend so you can assert what got sent and to whom, and Action Mailbox for receiving and routing incoming mail to a handler that looks and feels like a controller. I don't expect that from a web framework, and I'm genuinely not sure I'd reach for it in production over something like Mailchimp once you need real deliverability guarantees, but the fact that it's there, and that the testing story around it is this good, is the kind of "we already thought of that" touch that kept showing up throughout the book.

Internationalization was the same story from a different angle. I've done i18n on a large JEE app, and wrapping calls in `t()` helpers is a bit of a chore, but I've done worse to a frontend. Where Rails wins is having everything, including ActiveRecord's own validation error messages, live in the same YAML files instead of splitting "your" translations from the framework's built-in ones the way Java properties files tend to. It's not a radically different idea than properties files, but having one format and one place for all of it is a real convenience.

## Views: ERB earns the light touch

I consider my team's move to JSF a step backward, and reading through Rails' view layer reinforced why. A webpage mostly needs HTML with a little programmatic output sprinkled in, and ERB is happy to be exactly that if you use it with a light touch, the same way a JSP is fine if you don't let it turn into a Java class wearing an HTML costume. The book even includes the same warning I'd give: don't push logic into the view, keep it DRY, push the smart stuff back into the model or controller. Rails gives you the option to step away from raw HTML entirely with something like Slim, which the book covers and which genuinely looks like an upgrade in conciseness, but I deliberately stayed away from it. Once you leave plain HTML behind you sometimes lose the ability to just reach in and do the thing you want, and having direct access to HTML is one of the places JSF shoots itself in the foot.

Turbo and Stimulus impressed me more than I expected. As a longtime JSF developer, I'm instinctively wary of any tool that promises to let you avoid writing JavaScript, because usually what you get instead is a worse, more indirect version of JavaScript. Turbo and Stimulus don't try to hide JavaScript from you, they make it a first-class citizen of the app without requiring you to write mountains of it. That's a real needle to thread and Rails threads it well.

## Testing that doesn't punish you for having a lot of it

This is the area where the JEE contrast was sharpest. Heavyweight containers make it genuinely hard to fire things up and test without a slow, ponderous deployment cycle. Capybara spins up a lightweight server and drives headless Chrome quickly, and Minitest itself is fast enough that I now have hundreds of tests across the Storefront app and still don't think twice about running the whole suite after a change, or running just the subset relevant to what I'm working on. That combination, fast unit tests plus cheap full-stack system tests, is something I don't have on the JEE side.

The one asterisk: the system tests were occasionally flaky without retries, which brought back some uncomfortable memories of flaky Selenium and Geb tests in my Java app. Not a dealbreaker, but a reminder that browser-driven tests carry the same risk no matter what framework is running underneath them. On the philosophy side, I'm a BDD fan, so I liked that RSpec is a drop-in replacement for Minitest if you want it. I've wired Geb (Groovy) into a JEE app back when I was Groovy-curious, and I already love hspec in Haskell, so seeing Rails treat "swap your test framework" as a supported first-class option rather than a fight was a nice touch.

## ActiveRecord versus the POJO tax

Coming from Java, having models that know their own fields through reflection is close to a superpower. Add a new table on the JEE side and I'm stuck wiring up a verbose POJO, and often adding a new class into the middleware even when there's no actual business logic to enforce. IntelliJ can generate the getters and setters for you, but it's still a tax you pay on every table. A Rails model doesn't even make you define most of your fields, it just knows them from the schema, and it writes most of the migration for you in the first place. I assume most modern frameworks have caught up to something similar by now, but coming from the old-fashioned way of doing things, it's genuinely slick.

## Skinny middleware, and I mean that as a compliment

I appreciated that Rails doesn't insist on a heavy middleware layer. MVC is enough separation of concerns for most projects, including mine. I rarely have complicated rules that need enforcing in a middle tier, and in my JEE app that layer tends to be a passthrough that mostly repeats itself and translates values back and forth for no real benefit. Rails is comfortable letting the controller hold some logic, because that's what it's there for, or pushing it down to the model as the last line of enforcement before anything hits the database. That's a much better default than what I'm used to.

## Where this leaves me

Most of my reactions here are colored by the JEE lens I'm reading everything through, and I'll be the first to admit that most frameworks look good next to JEE. It's an old, overcomplicated, designed-by-committee way of building web apps, and it drags a lot of baggage that I wouldn't expect a modern framework to carry. Rails, on the other hand, lived up to its reputation. It's the all-inclusive option, and the flexibility holds up everywhere except right at the core. Try to rip out Active Record and you have to ask whether you're really still using Rails at that point, which feels like a fair trade.

What I want to find out next is what an all-inclusive framework actually buys you, and what you give up without one. Django is next on the list rather than indefinitely deferred, since it's the closest apples-to-apples comparison. After that I want to spend real time on a pure JavaScript frontend, and on the opposite end of the spectrum, something slimmed down like a Go microservice with a plain REST API, or a simple Compojure backend. Rails set a high bar for "batteries included done well." I'm curious how much of that I'll miss once the batteries aren't there.
